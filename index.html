01Approach

Uncertainty is highest in extreme environments. So is the opportunity for those who understand the risk.

Satellites see the surface but miss the gaps. Physical models capture the dynamics but drift. AI finds patterns but needs grounding. On their own, each falls short when conditions are extreme and changing fast.

82 NORTH brings them together into a single predictive system. Observations constrain the physics, physics keeps the AI honest, and every output carries a clear measure of uncertainty.

02Capabilities

Predict

Reconstruct what happened and forecast what comes next, from past seasons to hours and months ahead, blending satellite observations, physical models and machine learning.

Assess

Quantify uncertainty instead of hiding it. Know the range of what could happen, and how likely each outcome is. Use hindcasts to sharpen our understanding of past performance and enhance our next prediction.

Operate

Turn forecasts into decision-ready intelligence that keeps people, vessels and missions safe, delivered as layers and APIs that plug into the systems you already use.

03Fram

Fram: ice intelligence for polar navigation.

Our first operational layer. As the Arctic opens up to more traffic, vessels are entering waters where more fragmented and mobile sea ice imposes greater risk. Fram tells operators what is possible for their vessel class, now and in the seasons ahead.

Hours to days

Nowcast

Live ice risk for vessels underway: current conditions, near-term change, and which routes are open for a given vessel class.

Weeks to months

Seasonal

Probabilistic outlooks of ice conditions and access windows to support scheduling, chartering and mission planning.

Past seasons

Hindcast

Look back at routes and decisions: what the ice did, which alternatives existed, and what they meant for fuel, cost and risk.

04Nansen

Inspired by a bet on the ice.

In 1893, Fridtjof Nansen did something most thought reckless. Rather than fight the Arctic pack, he let his ship freeze into it. He had studied how debris from a wreck off Siberia had drifted across the Arctic Ocean to Greenland, and bet that the ice itself would carry him across the Arctic.

He had Fram built for that bet. Her rounded hull was designed so that the pressure of the ice would lift her up rather than crush her. She drifted for three years, reached higher latitudes than any ship before her, and came home intact. Nansen set out from her on skis and reached 86°14′N, further north than anyone had been.

That is the spirit behind 82 NORTH: understand the environment deeply enough that uncertainty becomes something you can work with, and put that understanding to work for the people who depend on it.
